INFOSYS:
The Sikka–Murthy Gray War

A chronicle of private scoreboards, invisible battle lines, and the moment when keeping the game moving forward became impossible.

No Heroes  •  No Villains  •  Only Moves
The Battlefield
Seven factions. One company. No shared scoreboard.

What follows is not a story of right and wrong. It is a geopolitical map of a corporate entity at war with itself — where the CEO, the founder, the board, the CFO, the legal counsel, the whistleblower, and the shareholders each operated on intelligence the others could not see.

Read it like a field report. Every quote is an intercepted transmission. Every number is a casualty. Every question is a trap.

The War Log
2014
JUNE 12, 2014
Sikka Camp

Infosys announces appointment of Vishal Sikka as first non-founder CEO & MD. Murthy, then Executive Chairman, says: "Vishal brings valuable experience as a leader of a large, global corporation. His illustrious track record and value system make him an ideal choice to lead Infosys."

Between 2013 and 2014, Infosys had seen at least 13 top-level exits.

OCTOBER 2015
First Blood

Rajiv Bansal resigns as CFO. Infosys signs a severance agreement to pay him ₹17.38 crore — equivalent to 24 months' pay — in tranches. First tranche of ₹5.2 crore paid immediately.

This was unprecedented in Infosys history. The norm was 3 months. The board later admitted the payment was excessive and that "an element of subjectivity had influenced the quantum."

If you are Rajiv Bansal, what is your private scoreboard when you sign that severance agreement — and what do you think the board believes your scoreboard is?
→ Quartz: Arbitration → Times of India
DECEMBER 2015
Shadow Move

David Kennedy, General Legal Counsel, resigns. Receives a special 12-month severance of nearly $1 million, despite having an existing employment contract.

If you are on the compensation committee, why does the legal counsel who just resigned get a special severance that no one else gets — and who else in the room knows about it?
→ Quartz
2016
FEBRUARY 2016
Sikka Camp

The Infosys board raises Sikka's compensation by 55% to $11 million per year (from $7.08 million). Breakdown: $1M base, $3M variable, $2M RSUs, $5M stock options.

The shareholder vote: Only 23.57% of promoter (founder) votes are cast in favor. The founders vote against their own CEO's compensation.

"I am not disappointed. I don't care. I have 100% backing of the board and support from the shareholders… I still respect the founders. Why they did this, you should ask them." — Vishal Sikka
If you are Narayana Murthy, what do you see when you look at a $11M pay package for a CEO while your engineers get 80% variable pay — and why can't the board see it?

2017
FEBRUARY 2017
Murthy Camp

Murthy gives a scathing interview to Economic Times:

"We have seen a concerning drop in governance standards at Infosys. Let me illustrate this with just one example. Providing huge severance pay (with 100% variable) to some departing employees while giving only 80% variable for employees in the company is one such example. Such payments raise doubts whether the company is using such payments as hush money to hide something."

Same month: Anonymous whistleblower letters sent to SEBI and US SEC alleging wrongdoing in the Panaya acquisition. Claims include: Panaya was overvalued; relatives of top Infosys officials had invested in Panaya indirectly through VC funds; former CFO Rajiv Bansal was not in favor of the deal; the acquisition may have benefited insiders.

If you are the whistleblower, what is the one piece of intelligence you have that makes you certain the board's investigation will never find what you found?

Murthy releases a 1,600+ word letter to "Advisors" and his July 8 letter to the board. Key excerpts:

"What do some well-meaning shareholders and I want from the Infosys board? Not money, not position for our children and not power. We just do not want the board to drive this institution to death through serious governance deficits in our own life time."
"The best way to ensure longevity of any corporation is by pursing good governance. Businesses will go through ups and downs and that is par for the course; but bad governance is not."

I have not commented about his strategy or its execution since I have not seen him operate from the vantage point of an Infosys board member. "Somehow, public has been made to think by some vested interests that this is a fight between Vishal and us, the founders and the early adopters. That is simply not true."
"The general belief among a large number of shareholders is that the current attitude of the board is a clear example of the worst board governance in India's corporate history."

SEPTEMBER 18, 2018
The Verdict

Arbitration tribunal rules in favor of Rajiv Bansal. Infosys ordered to pay the remaining ₹12.17 crore of the ₹17.38 crore severance. The sole arbitrator — former Supreme Court judge R.V. Raveendran — dismisses Infosys' charges of breach of confidentiality and data deletion. Rejects Infosys' ₹143 crore counter-claim. Orders 12% interest per annum from January 1, 2018.

"While the award acknowledges that Infosys had bona fide disputes, its counter-claim for refund of previously paid severance amount of Rs 5.2 crore and damages, has been rejected." — Infosys

The Gray Zone — No One Was Wrong
Intelligence Assessment: All Factions Rational
Sikka Was Not Wrong
He was trying to transform a body-shopping IT services firm into an AI-first technology company. He pushed automation, design thinking, and product-led growth. He gave iPhones to 3,000 employees. He let people wear jeans. He acquired Panaya and Skava to build IP. He was the highest-paid CEO in Indian IT because the board believed he was worth it. He was scoring on technological transformation and market relevance.
Murthy Was Not Wrong
He had built Infosys on "compassionate capitalism," transparency, and values. A ₹17.38 crore severance to a departing CFO when employees got only 80% variable pay violated everything Infosys stood for. A $200M acquisition with whistleblower allegations of insider benefit demanded full disclosure — not a summary press release. The board's refusal to release investigation details was the opposite of the "when in doubt, disclose" culture he had built. He was scoring on founder values, transparency, and institutional identity.
The Board Was Not Wrong
They had hired lawyers (Gibson Dunn, Cyril Amarchand Mangaldas, Latham & Watkins). Three independent investigations found no evidence of wrongdoing. They had a fiduciary duty to protect the company from reputational damage. Releasing every email and every conversation would destroy executive privilege and make future CEOs impossible to hire. They were scoring on legal protection and fiduciary duty.
Bansal Was Not Wrong
He had a signed contract. The arbitrator agreed. He got his ₹17.38 crore. He was scoring on contractual entitlement.
The Whistleblower Was Not Wrong
The allegations were specific, detailed, and involved named people and named transactions. They were scoring on accountability and institutional integrity.
The Assessment
Everyone was scoring on a different board. There was no right answer. There was only the move that ended Sikka's game.

Q1. The ICICI Bank board gave Kochhar a clean chit in March 2018, expressing "full confidence" and ruling out any quid pro quo. The Srikrishna enquiry (Jun 2018 – Jan 2019) found "gross / serious violations" of the Code of Conduct. The board then unanimously resolved "termination for cause" on 30 Jan 2019. Did the board lie in March, or did it learn something new between March and January?

Q2. The whistleblower complaint was made in October 2016. The board gave a clean chit in March 2018. The CBI filed a PE in March 2018. If the board had access to the same information as the whistleblower for 17 months, was its clean chit an act of due diligence or institutional self-preservation?

Q3. Kochhar's early retirement was accepted on 4 Oct 2018. She exercised 6,90,000 ESOPs and received benefits under that letter. On 30 Jan 2019, the bank revoked the retirement letter and treated her separation as "termination for cause" retroactive to 4 Oct 2018. The Bombay High Court (2022) found the bank had no legal basis to terminate a non-existent employment. If the court is right, was the bank's action vindictive or procedurally creative?

Q4. The CBI FIR states that on 8 Sep 2009 — one day after the Rs 300 Cr loan was disbursed to Videocon International Electronics Ltd — V.N. Dhoot transferred Rs 64 Cr to NuPower Renewables (Deepak Kochhar's firm) via Supreme Energy. The tribunal (Jul 2025) confirmed this as quid pro quo. If the transfer happened one day later, is that evidence of planning or evidence of coincidence?

Q5. K.V. Kamath (Kochhar's predecessor) and Sandeep Bakhshi (her successor) were both on the loan sanctioning committee. The CBI named only Kochhar, Deepak Kochhar, and V.N. Dhoot in the FIR. If the same committee approved the loans, why were only three people accused while the rest of the committee became witnesses?

Q6. The Kochhars acquired a flat in Churchgate for Rs 11 lakh in Oct 2016, while the flat was valued at Rs 5.25 Cr in 1996. The CBI alleges this was another gratification. If a property transaction happens at 2% of its 20-year-old valuation, is that a gift, a distressed sale, or a typo in the paperwork?

CBI FIR (24 Jan 2019) ▶
CBI | FIR against Chanda Kochhar, Deepak Kochhar, V.N. Dhoot

Sections Applied: IPC 120B (criminal conspiracy), IPC 420 (cheating), Prevention of Corruption Act Section 7 (taking gratification), Section 13(2) read with 13(1)(d) (criminal misconduct / pecuniary advantage).

The Quid Pro Quo Allegation:

"On 26 August 2009, a Rupee Term Loan of Rs 300 crore was sanctioned to Videocon International Electronics Limited (VIEL) by the ICICI Bank. Out of the Rs 300 crore sanctioned to VIEL, Rs 64 crore was transferred by Dhoot to NuPower Renewables from Videocon Industries via his other firm, Supreme Energy. Thus Chanda Kochhar got illegal gratification/undue benefit through her husband from Videocon Industries/VN Dhoot for sanctioning rupee term loan of Rs 300 crore to VIEL."

Additional Loans: Millennium Appliances India (Rs 175 Cr, Jun 2009), Sky Appliances (Rs 240 Cr, Nov 2010), Applicomp India (Rs 300 Cr, May 2011), Videocon Industries (Rs 750 Cr, Oct 2011). Total: Rs 1,875 Cr. All declared NPAs by 2017. Bank loss: Rs 1,730 Cr.

The Flat: Kochhar family trust acquired Churchgate flat for Rs 11 lakh in Oct 2016; flat valued at Rs 5.25 Cr in 1996.

BOMBAY HIGH COURT ORDER (10 Nov 2022) ▶
Bombay High Court | Justice R.I. Chagla | Suit No. 114 of 2022 & Suit No. 313 of 2020

Core Issue: Whether ICICI Bank, having accepted Kochhar's request for early retirement, could subsequently treat her services as "termination for cause" with effect from the date of acceptance of early retirement.

Kochhar's Position:

"Mr. Chinoy has submitted that ICICI Bank's purported decision dated 30th January, 2019 to terminate Mrs. Kochhar's (non existent) employment for cause, with all attendant consequences including revocation of all the retirement benefits to which Mrs. Kochhar was entitled under the Retirement Agreement dated 4th October, 2018 was wrongful and a clear breach by ICICI Bank."

Bank's Position: The 4 Oct 2018 letter explicitly stated that certain benefits would be decided by the Board only upon conclusion of the Enquiry. The Enquiry found gross violations. Therefore, the retirement acceptance was conditional and revocable.

Court's Observation on Banking Regulation Act:

"As per the terms of Section 10B(9) of the Banking Regulation Act, the maximum permissible four months period for such an interim arrangement was to expire on 17th October, 2018. The Board would, therefore, not have been in a position to accept any further extension of Mrs. Kochhar's leave beyond 17th October, 2018."

The court noted that at the relevant time, the only option available to ICICI Bank was to accept Kochhar's request for early retirement, since the bank could neither ask her to resume duties nor terminate her pending the Enquiry.

Srikrishna ENQUIRY REPORT (27 Jan 2019) ▶
Justice B.N. Srikrishna (Retd.) | Commissioned by ICICI Bank Board | Jun 2018 – Jan 2019

Findings (per Bombay HC Order referencing the Report):

Mrs. Kochhar had committed "Gross / Serious Violations" of the Code of Conduct for extended periods of time.

Violations included:

— Failure to disclose to the bank's board about her husband's business associations with the Videocon group, which was a customer of the bank.

— Continuing to be part of committees that sanctioned credit facilities to Videocon when she should have recused herself on grounds of conflict of interest.

— Violation of the bank's Code of Conduct, Framework for dealing with conflict of interest, Deeds for Covenants, and Clawback agreement.

— Violation of Companies Act 1956/2013, Banking Regulation Act 1949, SEBI Listing Regulations 2015, and RBI Master Circular on Loans.

SAFEMA APPELLATE TRIBUNAL ORDER (3 Jul 2025) ▶
Appellate Tribunal under SAFEMA | Order Date: 3 July 2025

Key Finding:

"An appellate tribunal under SAFEMA has found former ICICI Bank CEO Chanda Kochhar guilty of accepting a bribe of Rs 64 crore. This significant payment was identified as a quid pro quo for sanctioning a Rs 300 crore loan to the Videocon Group in 2009."

ED Position Upheld: The Rs 64 Cr constituted proceeds of crime under PMLA. The tribunal reinforced ED's provisional attachment of Kochhars' properties.

Previous Order Overturned: An earlier November 2020 order that had released Kochhar's assets worth Rs 78 Cr was deemed flawed.

Confirmation: The tribunal found a direct link between loan disbursement (7 Sep 2009) and the Rs 64 Cr transfer (8 Sep 2009) to NuPower Renewables via Supreme Energy.

The Gray Zone: Nobody Was Wrong

Chanda Kochhar was not wrong to join a bank as a trainee in 1984 and rise to become its CEO over 25 years. She was not wrong to sit on a loan sanctioning committee where the consortium structure meant multiple banks approved the same loan. She was not wrong to believe that her husband's business dealings were separate from her professional obligations — until a tribunal said they weren't. She was not wrong to accept early retirement when the law required the bank to either let her resume or let her leave. She was not wrong to exercise ESOPs that were vested under a letter the bank itself issued.

The ICICI Bank board was not wrong to give a clean chit in March 2018 based on the information available at the time. A board's duty is to act on evidence, not on rumours. When the Srikrishna enquiry produced a different picture, the board was not wrong to change its position. A board that cannot change its mind when new facts emerge is a board that cannot govern. The clawback of Rs 7.41 Cr in bonuses was not wrong — it was contractual, agreed to in the clawback agreement Kochhar herself had signed.

The CBI was not wrong to file an FIR when a whistleblower complaint, bank records, and a one-day gap between loan disbursement and fund transfer suggested a pattern. The ED was not wrong to register a PMLA case when money moved from a borrower to a CEO's husband's company within 24 hours. The SAFEMA tribunal was not wrong to confirm the quid pro quo in July 2025 — it reviewed evidence over years.

The Bombay High Court was not wrong to question whether a bank can terminate someone who has already retired. Contract law does not evaporate because the subject is famous. If the bank accepted her retirement, the relationship ended. What the bank did on 30 Jan 2019 was not termination — it was revocation of a settlement, dressed in the language of employment law.

Arvind Gupta was not wrong to blow the whistle in 2016. A shareholder who sees Rs 64 Cr move from a borrower to a CEO's spouse within 24 hours of a Rs 300 Cr loan disbursement is not a troublemaker. He is a citizen.

The only wrong was the assumption that a bank CEO's family could do business with her bank's borrowers and nobody would draw a line between the two.
AMBIGUITY RESERVE // questions that outlive answers

Q7. The Srikrishna enquiry found "gross violations" but was an internal fact-finding exercise. The CBI filed an FIR based on its own investigation. The tribunal confirmed quid pro quo. If three different bodies looked at the same facts and reached the same conclusion at different times, was the delay evidence of thoroughness or evidence of a system that moves slowly when the accused is powerful?

Q8. K.V. Kamath and Sandeep Bakhshi were on the same loan committee as Kochhar. Kamath was her predecessor; Bakhshi her successor. Both became CEO of ICICI Bank. If the same committee approved the loans, and only Kochhar is accused of conspiracy, is the conspiracy a legal fact or a narrative convenience that requires only one villain?

Q9. The bank clawed back Rs 7.41 Cr in bonuses but the alleged loss to the bank is Rs 1,730 Cr. If the penalty for a Rs 1,730 Cr loss is Rs 7.41 Cr, what is the exchange rate between corporate misconduct and personal financial consequence? And if Kochhar is eventually convicted, does the bank get the Rs 1,730 Cr back?

Q10. Kochhar's defence includes that she was not aware of her husband's business associations. The tribunal found the funds were routed to her husband's company and "invalidated her defence of ignorance." If a CEO does not know what her spouse's company receives, is that ignorance or plausible deniability — and is there a legal difference between the two?

Q11. The bank gave a clean chit, then commissioned an enquiry, then accepted retirement, then revoked it, then terminated for cause, then clawed back bonuses, then cooperated with CBI/ED/SFIO. If an institution changes its position five times on the same employee, is that due process or institutional panic?

Q12. The whistleblower wrote to the Prime Minister in 2016. Nothing happened for 17 months. The board gave a clean chit. Then another whistleblower emerged in 2018. Then everything happened at once. If justice requires two whistleblowers to move a system that one could not, what does that say about the threshold of noise required to make power accountable?
